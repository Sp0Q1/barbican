How scoping works

A pentest is a box

It has a height, a width and a depth. Height is how much you put in it. Depth is how much of the machinery you let us see. Width — the time — is not something you pick first; it follows from the other two.

Height — how much goes in

Testing every release used to be arithmetic that did not work: a human rereading the same codebase every fortnight is unaffordable, so everyone settled for once a year. Machines are extraordinary at reading all of it and never getting bored, and terrible at knowing which bug matters to your business. Splitting the work along that line is what makes continuous testing possible at a price worth paying — and a human still puts their name on every finding.

What the AI layer does

  • Reads every route, handler and IaC file in scope — not the sample a human would have time for.
  • Maps the authorisation matrix across roles and flags the combinations nobody documented.
  • Drafts and mutates payloads against candidate injection points, then triages the noise.
  • Replays the whole finding set against each new build, so a regressed fix gets caught.
  • Writes the first draft of the reproduction steps. The tester rewrites what is wrong.

What it never does

  • Exploit anything without a named human approving the action against the signed scope.
  • Touch production data, or any system outside the agreed rules of engagement.
  • Put a finding in your report unverified — every one is reproduced by hand first.
  • Reach a model outside the EU without your prior written approval.
  • Replace the tester. It buys back the days they would have spent on grep.

Authorisation bypass on the admin order endpoint

Raised 4 March · reproduced by hand · retested 11 March

What it is
Any authenticated user can read and modify orders belonging to other customers by changing a single identifier in the request path.
Technical detail
The handler checks that a session exists but never that the session owns the order. GET /api/orders/{id} returns any record; PUT accepts writes to it. A standard customer account is enough.
Impact
Every order in the system — names, addresses, contents and totals — is readable by anyone who can register. Order contents can be altered before fulfilment.
What to do
Authorise on ownership, not on authentication: resolve the order through the session's customer rather than trusting the identifier. The same pattern appears on three other routes, listed in finding 17.

Before you ask

The questions
everyone asks

Will testing break production?

It is the first thing we agree and it is written into the rules of engagement. Most continuous work runs against staging or a production-like environment; where we do test production, destructive checks are excluded by name, we agree a window, and you get a phone number that reaches the tester during it. If something does go wrong we stop and tell you immediately.

Who actually does the testing?

A named human, and on the continuous tiers the same one across the year — which is the point. A tester who already knows your stack spends their time on your authorisation logic instead of relearning your architecture every quarter. You will know their name before the engagement starts.

What happens if you find something critical mid-engagement?

You hear about it that day, by phone, before it is written up. Nobody sits on a critical finding until the report is tidy. The write-up follows; the warning does not wait for it.

Where does our data go?

The platform is EU-hosted and each engagement runs in its own isolated tenant, wiped at closeout. Some frontier testing models are not yet available in the EU — those are opt-in, named in your engagement letter, and never a default. If you would rather we use nothing outside the EU, say so and we will not.

Do you sign NDAs?

Yes, routinely, and we will sign yours rather than insisting on ours. We also work codename-only where you want it: nothing in our infrastructure has to carry your company name.

What if you do not find anything?

Then you get a report that says so, with the evidence of what was tested and how — which is the thing your customer or auditor actually wants to see. It happens, and it is a good outcome. We will also tell you honestly whether a deeper level of access would have been likely to find more.

How much access should we give you?

More than feels comfortable, usually. Blackbox is the most realistic and the least efficient — you pay for the days we spend discovering what you could have told us in an afternoon. Greybox is the sweet spot for most teams. See the box at the top of this page; it is the same conversation we will have on the call.

Can you start next week?

Often, yes. Scoping is a conversation, not a procurement process, and we schedule around your deadline rather than our quarter. Tell us the date you are working to. If we cannot meet it we will say so on the first call rather than in week three.

We need this for a customer or an auditor. Does that work?

Yes. You get a report, a management summary, and a letter of attestation your customer or auditor can accept — plus the retest, so what you hand over describes the fixed system rather than the broken one.
